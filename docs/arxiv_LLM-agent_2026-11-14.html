Where LLM Agents Get Stuck Debugging Databases: Access More Than Reasoning

Authors:Gustavo Pinto
Date:2026-11-12 18:56:22

Large language model (LLM) coding agents are increasingly asked not only to write code but to debug running systems from live runtime data. We report a field observation of one such agent (Claude Code) as it diagnosed eight database incidents in a production message-dispatch engine. In every incident whose root cause depended on runtime concurrency, the agent announced a cause, shipped a fix, and later walked it back, and each wrong guess cost a deploy-and-observe cycle. Mapping each incident to what the agent lacked, we find that access to production state was the most pervasive bottleneck. The missing access included live lock views, untruncated deadlock reports, read-only execution, and an explicit environment context. An access gap was present in all five concurrency-dependent incidents: alone in one, and in four combined with a reasoning error such as anchoring on an early cause, incomplete exploration of the configuration, or a misreading of the code's transaction semantics. The three incidents that did not depend on concurrency needed nothing beyond what the agent already had. Within the incidents, the agent's wrong hypotheses were consistent with the evidence it could see; they were not fabrications. We argue that the next step for production-debugging agents is a forensics layer that gives the agent the access it lacks inside existing security controls. Such a layer would combine a safe interrogation API, a lock-and-deadlock flight recorder, and a production replay harness.

LiveGeo: Benchmarking Geographic Agents in Continuously Evolving Worlds

Authors:Pengcheng Zhou, Xuanyu Liu, Yanchen Yin, Tianjie Ju, Bobo Li, Shengqiong Wu, Hao Fei, Mong-Li Lee, Wynne Hsu
Date:2026-11-12 18:45:58

We introduce GeoWorld, a controllable and continuously evolving geographic environment platform for LLM agents, and LiveGeo, a benchmark for evaluating reliable decision-making in real-world geographic environments using GeoWorld. Unlike existing evaluations that largely reason and plan over fixed geographic states, GeoWorld is grounded in real-world geographic structure and allows external world states to evolve along their own temporal processes while preserving verifiable state histories. LiveGeo places geographic tasks within this evolving environment, requiring agents to actively acquire task-relevant information, verify whether the evidence supporting prior decisions remains valid, and revise their plans when necessary. Our results reveal that even frontier LLMs struggle with static tasks, with no model consistently excelling across tasks, while strong static performance does not ensure dynamic reliability. Further analysis identifies a key bottleneck: agents struggle to acquire and translate relevant evidence into valid decisions and maintain their correctness as the world evolves. By releasing LiveGeo together with its GeoWorld platform, we aim to provide the community with a flexible and extensible infrastructure for studying the next generation of agents capable of reliable action in evolving geographic worlds.

Scalable Conversational Assessment: Chat-Based Exams with a Two-Agent LLM Pipeline

Authors:Geoffrey Challen, Craig Zilles
Date:2026-11-12 18:16:37

Oral exams are compelling because (1) students can be asked to provide their reasoning for an answer and (2) their interactive nature permits follow-up questions to resolve ambiguous answers. But oral exams scale poorly. In this paper, we present scalable conversational assessment, an LLM-based system that conducts interactive text-based conversations with students to assess their ability to make and support an argument. We use a two-agent architecture to separate concerns and reduce the likelihood that answers are leaked to the student: the interviewer interacts directly with the student, but only receives guidance about the next question to ask the student; the hidden evaluator knows the goals of the whole activity, tracks the student's progress using a rubric, and guides the interviewer on when to follow-up on the current question or proceed to the next question. The system was first tested using a collection of simulated students with LLM personas of a particular ability level or adversarial tactic (e.g., answer-extractors, prompt-injectors, confident fabricators). A novel element of this evaluation was that it required the use of a fictional assessment topic to prevent personas that shouldn't have information from using knowledge present in the LLM's pre-training. The system was also deployed in a small, elective AI literacy course in both formative and summative contexts. Through manual and post-facto AI review of the transcripts we identified opportunities to improve the follow-up question generation and ensure that grading only credited ideas that the student (and not the interviewer) contributed.

Actor, Judge, or Teacher? Understanding Decision Models in Long-Horizon Agents

Authors:Haochen Liu, Qianhao Zhu, Zixuan Wang, Dingming Li, Tongbo Chen, Peng Qiu, Yingqi Qu, Weiming Lu, Jun Xiao, Yongliang Shen
Date:2026-11-12 17:42:46

Decision models answer a question with a typed output, such as a choice among options with a probability for each, so one model can choose an agent's actions, evaluate actions already taken, or score every action to teach a policy with reinforcement learning. Prior work has studied them mainly as actors, judging their usefulness by how well they act. We ask whether a decision model that acts poorly can still teach well. We compare three decision models and three general LLMs as actors and judges on ALFWorld, and use JEV as a teacher on ALFWorld and WebShop. As actors, all six models are pulled away from a subgoal that must come first by an easier one. Discovering Effective Agent Memory Designs through Program Evolution

Authors:Xinheng Ding, Seyyed Saeid Cheshmi, Ziyang Zhang, Tairan Chen, Wenya Xie, Yu Hou, Rui Zhang, Zirui Liu
Date:2026-11-12 16:42:04

Program evolution provides a promising approach to discover agent memory designs and study the choices underlying their effectiveness. We introduce MAPE, a framework that evolves whole memory programs from minimal seeds through a standardized lifecycle interface and an iterative diagnosis-proposal loop. Experiments on LoCoMo and LongMemEval yield an absolute accuracy gain of up to 12% over manually designed baselines at 35% of the serving cost, with further evaluations supporting transfer across serving backbones and benchmarks. Empirical analyses and ablations identify and validate two recurring design patterns: retaining raw conversations as retrievable evidence preserves details omitted from extracted memories, while composing retrieved evidence according to query intent helps the answering model use it effectively. Transplanting the composition module into five existing memory systems improves accuracy across all five. Beyond these patterns, the search objective shapes where computation goes: optimizing serving cost alongside accuracy shifts computation from LLM-based memory construction to selective processing at query time while retaining competitive accuracy. Together, these findings offer empirical lessons for agent memory design. Code is available at https://github.com/ash-ding/MAPE.

We introduce a benchmark that injects objective, localisable errors of four types (general issues, mathematics, numerics, citations) at three severity levels into real papers and measures each reviewer's detection rate by error type and severity, together with its false-positive rate on clean copies. Skill Adapter: Personalized Modification and Online Routing of General-Purpose Skills

Authors:Qijia Zhuang, Yanyi Gou, Wenbin Ma, Yuxiang Ren
Date:2026-11-12 16:05:23

Large language model (LLM) agents use external skills to reuse task procedures and experience with tools. General-purpose skills describe shared execution procedures, while specific tool choices, operating practices, and output requirements vary across users. In long-horizon tasks, subgoals, available inputs, and environmental states change as execution progresses. Agents must select currently applicable skills from an expanding skill pool and prepare candidates for subsequent steps. We propose Skill Adapter, which enables personalized modification and online routing of general-purpose skills. The system distills local adjustments from user requirements and execution feedback and stores them as Adapters linked to general-purpose skills. The routing agent selects general-purpose skills and applicable Adapters based on the current subtask, execution progress, and environmental feedback, incrementally maintaining a task-level online skill library that the main LLM reads on demand. The system preloads candidates for subsequent nodes and rechecks their applicability once actual inputs are determined. Compared with SkillRL, it improves task success rates by an average of 11.43 percentage points and reduces mean execution time on all three benchmarks. Skill Adapter retains its performance advantage when the skill pool grows to 1,000 skills.

Outcome-Mass Alignment: Controlling Length-Dependent Trajectory Influence in Multi-Turn LLM Agent Reinforcement Learning

Authors:Dawei Zhang, Yubin Huangfu, Jessica Fan, Boyu Wei, Likang Wu, Hongke Zhao, Ming He, Zhu Teng
Date:2026-11-12 13:52:12

To keep long interaction contexts tractable, stepwise group-relative training for multi-turn LLM agents materializes each source trajectory into multiple response rows and valid tokens. This expansion creates an outer-accounting problem distinct from within-trajectory credit assignment: row-based outcome statistics can count one episode outcome multiple times, while token-level reduction can assign unequal aggregate objective mass across trajectories. We term these effects outcome-statistics multiplicity and objective-mass imbalance. We introduce Outcome-Mass Alignment (OMA), a two-component method that makes both accounting choices explicit. Outcome Alignment computes outcome statistics over unique source trajectories, while Mass Alignment controls aggregate objective mass after within-trajectory aggregation of optimizer-native token objectives. Neither redesigns the base optimizer's credit assignment, importance ratios, or clipping. Across matched ALFWorld GRPO comparisons at two model scales, where failed trajectories typically expand much more than successful ones, OMA improves matched-horizon endpoint success by 7.0-9.4 percentage points. Ordinary WebShop exhibits weak or reversed expansion-outcome coupling and correspondingly mixed effects, whereas in a success-oriented WebShop setting, WebShop-SOL, OMA improves success nAUC from 0.399 to 0.511. Component ablations and GiGPO and GSPO comparisons further support component separability and cross-optimizer compatibility. These results show that trajectory expansion always defines an implicit weighting rule, but its practical effect depends on how expansion couples to outcomes and optimizer-native updates.

When Expert Disagreement Hurts: Prestige and Selectivity in LLM Decision Revision

Authors:Yupeng Tang, Mingfeng Lin
Date:2026-11-12 12:11:03

When language models revise a decision after another agent disagrees, neither reversal counts nor final accuracy shows whether they are fixing mistakes or abandoning correct answers. We introduce a controlled two-pass audit that holds the question, the evidence, and the model's initial answer fixed and varies only the revision context: neutral reflection, anonymous disagreement, or disagreement attributed to a domain expert. For binary decisions the accounting is exact: revision raises accuracy only if it is selective enough, reversing wrong answers more than $a/(1-a)$ times as often as correct ones, where $a$ is initial accuracy (nine times at 90%). Across five models and 1,989 evidence-grounded decisions in medicine, science, and law, no model clears this bar under expert disagreement, and accuracy falls by up to 16.1 points. The expert label, a prestige cue, adds reversals for four of the five models, yet its effect on accuracy ranges from a 5.2-point loss to a 2.4-point gain, so reversal counts are a poor proxy for harm. With identical arguments from both sources, the label still raises reversal for three proprietary models and in a pooled analysis of four newer models. Models also respond to stated source reliability. Revision pipelines should therefore be evaluated with paired, conditional metrics, per model and protocol.

Mobile GUI agents read the screen of a phone and operate apps on a user's behalf to complete multi-step tasks such as online shopping or messaging. Because the agent acts on whatever the screen shows, an attacker can embed malicious instructions into ordinary UI content and redirect its actions. We refer to this threat as environmental injection attack (EIA). Existing benchmarks measure this risk by pinning hand-crafted payloads to a fixed step of a fixed task.
