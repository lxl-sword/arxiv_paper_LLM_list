GraphNIAH: A Benchmark for Long-Context Reasoning over Real-World Graphs

Authors:Minh-Nam Tran, Abulhair Saparov
Date:2026-10-30 13:07:47

The needle-in-a-haystack (NIAH) benchmark was introduced as a test for measuring a model's ability to find small relevant facts within a very large body of input text. However, existing NIAH benchmarks each suffer from one or more of the following limitations: (1) reliance on synthetic data, so their evaluations may not generalize to real-world settings; (2) reuse of previously published datasets, leading to the risk of benchmark contamination; (3) dependence on manual annotation, which requires human effort to create and update data and is prone to future benchmark contamination; and (4) unrealistic simplification of long-context multi-hop reasoning where each next step is trivial given previous steps, while real-world reasoning often requires planning over multiple possible proof paths. To address these limitations, we introduce \Benchmark, a dynamic benchmark built from real citation relationships among scientific papers published after the evaluated models' knowledge cutoff date, which utilizes real-world information while ensuring no benchmark contamination. The fully automated benchmark construction pipeline samples representative subgraphs of the citation graph formed by these papers and converts each subgraph into a long-context QA example with a verifiable answer. We use GraphNIAH to evaluate eight LLMs from the GPT-5.6, Gemini-3, and Qwen3.5 families. We find that accuracy falls as questions require longer reasoning paths: at question depths of 16 and 32, the GPT-5.6 and Qwen3.5 models answer YES/NO questions no better than random guessing, mostly by answering NO; four of the eight models answer no counting example correctly, and the others model get worse as the subgraph size increases. These findings suggest that current LLMs often stop searching the citation relationships in their context before they reach the answer, even though every fact they need is in the context.

Date:2026-10-30 12:38:41

Over the past decades, query cardinality estimation on relational databases has been proven crucial, enabling the result size estimation of all sub-plans of each query. This allowed query optimizers to design efficient query plans, towards minimizing the query response time. Lately, knowledge graphs are being preferred over traditional databases in various applications, due to their ability to represent complex relationships and semantic meaning in a domain-specific context. In this work, we present a novel approach for supervised similarity-aware cardinality estimation on knowledge graphs that incorporates similarities into the model training process, thus treating them as first-class citizens. Authors:Mahmoud S. Mayaleh, Fetia Bannour, Nour-El-Houda Yellas
Date:2026-10-30 10:58:06

Deploying 6G network slices across administrative domains requires an online decision for each arriving request: which domain hosts each virtual network function, how traffic crosses domain boundaries, and whether the slice is admitted. This joint decision problem is NP-hard under continuous arrivals. Experiments on a parametrized family of multi-domain topologies show that no fixed planning strategy dominates across all regimes: the performance ranking reverses between topology classes, and feasible placements exist for most rejected requests but remain undiscovered. These findings identify the plan layer as the decisive performance lever. The EU Platform Work Directive, to be transposed by December 2026, requires ride-hailing platforms to state in plain language the main parameters of their automated decisions and how a worker's behaviour affects them. Such rules are exactly what a large language model (LLM) can read, so drivers will be able to let one decide for them. We compare two main families of rebalancing policy once their rules are public: a reactive policy (R) that sends idle drivers to cover requests just lost, and an anticipatory policy (A) that plans moves on a demand forecast. Date:2026-10-29 22:32:01

Typically, the LLM picks these calls by trial and error, with no guarantee of an executable plan. We present Capability-Grounded Agentic Planning (CGAP), which instead reasons about what a request requires and what tools provide. CGAP compiles the tool catalog deterministically into a PDDL model, uses an LLM to turn the request into goals, and solves the model with a classical planner. The communication graph of a fixed workflow restricts which agents can read each other's outputs: evidence retrieved by one agent may never reach the agent that needs it, leaving an information gap and an incomplete or incorrect answer. Adding a connection can deliver the missing evidence, but can also lead the receiver to adopt an incorrect upstream conclusion that displaces a correct answer; the final output alone does not separate the two. LLM-based agents are increasingly used to complete complex, long-horizon tasks without human intervention. PrivacyFlow: Privacy in AI Agents through Information Flow Analysis and Consequence-Aware Risk Management

Authors:Robin Staab, Tom Wollschläger, Vahab Mirrokni, Reza Shokri
Date:2026-10-29 01:54:36

Autonomous LLM agents executing multi-step workflows over personal data often misalign with user privacy expectations and leak sensitive, out-of-context information. Although Contextual Integrity (CI) formalizes this problem, existing benchmarks and defenses rarely operationalize CI for realistic multi-step task sequences in evolving environments. In this work, we first introduce agentic PrivacyBench, a benchmark of 5873 tasks in 986 task sequences with dynamic environment updates and CI-grounded allow-sets for sensitive information. We also introduce PrivacyFlow, a defense that separates deliberative privacy planning (System 2) from task execution (System 1), treating each sharing decision as a structured risk-management problem. Before execution, PrivacyFlow traces the provenance and purpose of each planned data flow, models recipient background knowledge, and simulates what external actors could infer. This allows us to model the downstream consequences of disclosure, including inference attacks and context-dependent harms. It then weighs the remaining disclosure risk against task utility and executes a minimized plan, blocks it, or consults the user. Across six models, PrivacyFlow significantly reduces step-level privacy failures and raises the share of completed task sequences without a detected privacy violation by 22--55 percentage points. We further highlight a new privacy--utility--autonomy tradeoff: allowing PrivacyFlow to proactively prompt the user in case of uncertainty can increase authorized task completion at the cost of shifting decision effort back to the user. Our results suggest that proactive CI reasoning can substantially improve agentic privacy and motivate further study of this tradeoff.

Large language models pass most functional-correctness benchmarks, but passing tests says little about whether their code is readable, efficient or secure. Because this responsibility may change as interactions unfold, we examine epistemic proactivity as a developing process. We conceptualize epistemic proactivity through self-regulated learning (SRL) moves, complemented by help-seeking strategies and manifestations of epistemic vigilance. Embodied instruction following in interactive environments requires agents to interpret natural language commands and complete long-horizon tasks. Existing planners either rely on large labeled trajectories, or use few-shot and zero-shot LLM prompting that produces a whole plan in a single serial reasoning process. These approaches depend on linguistic common sense, commit to a plan from the initial instruction, and only update after failures, which weakens robustness and state awareness. We present AtomTree, a hierarchical framework for state-aware task planning with LLMs. AtomTree incrementally builds a tree of subgoals and actions, where the LLM decides at each step, from the current symbolic state, whether to further decompose or to output a grounded low-level action. When a low-level action stalls, AtomTree triggers hierarchical backtracking replanning to return to the parent node and regenerate the subtree plan from the latest state, preventing continued drift along an incorrect trajectory.
